α-MnTe is one of the most exciting materials in magnetism right now. It is an altermagnet, a recently recognized kind of magnet whose spins cancel out overall yet still split its electrons by spin. For years, its Raman spectrum has shown two strong peaks that theory says should not be there, and the community has argued over what they mean. I led a study, together with collaborators across five institutions, to settle the question.

The answer turned out to be surprisingly ordinary: the peaks do not belong to MnTe at all. Within a minute of meeting air, oxygen pulls manganese up to the surface to form a thin oxide crust, leaving tellurium trapped just beneath it. The Raman laser then helps crystallize that tellurium, and the tellurium is what we see. Films we grew and measured without ever leaving vacuum, or protected with a thin cap, show none of these peaks.

The lesson reaches beyond one material. A few minutes in air can change what optical and surface measurements of tellurium-based quantum materials actually see. Careful handling is not a technicality. It decides whether you are studying the material or its rust.
