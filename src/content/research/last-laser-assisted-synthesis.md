Crystals just three atoms thick, such as MoS₂, promise smaller electronics and new kinds of optical devices. Growing them, however, is notoriously fiddly. Conventional methods mix separate metal and chalcogen sources with carrier and reaction gases, so every new material means long rounds of trial and error. The obvious shortcut, vaporizing the finished compound and letting it recrystallize, looked impossible: the powder needs around 1400 °C to vaporize, while the crystals grow best hundreds of degrees cooler.

During my PhD, I designed and ran the experiments for a way around this, which we called the laser-assisted synthesis technique, or LAST. A CO₂ laser heats a small graphite boat holding the powder until it vaporizes, while a furnace keeps the substrate a few millimeters above at a gentler growth temperature. Separating the two kinds of heat let us grow monolayers of four materials, MoS₂, MoSe₂, WS₂ and WSe₂, directly from their own powders in about two minutes, with crystals up to 100 micrometers across and no hydrogen or hazardous gases. Atomic-resolution microscopy confirmed clean, well-ordered crystals.

Because the starting material is simply the material itself, the growth recipes became predictable: compounds with stronger chemical bonds need more laser power and heat, a rule of thumb that cut our trial and error sharply. That simplicity is the point, offering a general and fast route to discovering new 2D quantum materials. LAST also became the foundation for my later work on time-resolved crystal growth and on tuning atomic vacancies during growth.
