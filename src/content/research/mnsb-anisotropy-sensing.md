MnSb is a magnet that stays magnetic far above room temperature, and like a grain of wood, it has preferred directions: its magnetism lines up more easily along some crystal axes than others. Crystal growers usually chase perfectly uniform films. I led a study that took the opposite route, growing MnSb on GaAs by molecular beam epitaxy so that it forms tiny crystalline islands pointing in two different directions, and then asking what that mix does to its magnetism.

The answer is a film with two built-in magnetic personalities. One family of islands lies flat and the other is tilted, and each prefers its own direction. Mapping the surface with magnetic force microscopy and a diamond quantum sensor revealed strong local magnetic fields, up to nearly 10 millitesla. Ferromagnetic resonance showed the clearest signature: rotating the applied field by 90 degrees shifts the film's response by about 500 millitesla, roughly 5 millitesla for every degree of tilt. The films kept this behavior after more than a year in open air.

What looks like a growth imperfection becomes a feature. A material this sensitive to field direction could work as a compact, low-cost compass for measuring both the strength and the direction of magnetic fields, or as a precise angle detector. And because each film grows its own unique mix of islands, each one carries a distinct magnetic fingerprint that could serve as a physical tag that is hard to copy.
