The surfaces of atomically thin crystals such as monolayer MoSe₂ are chemically shy: gas molecules and biomolecules barely stick, which is a poor trait for sensors. Missing atoms change that. A selenium vacancy gives a passing molecule something to grab onto, so the stronger the binding, the sharper the sensor's response. The catch is making vacancies on demand. The usual route (blasting a finished crystal with electron beams, ions, or plasma) etches it as it damages it, and conventional growth simply leaves vacancies wherever they happen to land.

I designed and ran experiments that grow the vacancies in from the start, using the laser-assisted synthesis technique I developed during my PhD. A CO₂ laser vaporizes stoichiometric MoSe₂ powder while a furnace holds the substrate at 700 °C. The twist is that selenium leaves the powder more easily than molybdenum: underdose the laser and selenium escapes first, leaving the crystal short of atoms; give it enough to sublimate as a whole and you get nearly pristine material. Two knobs do the tuning: the laser's energy, set by power and time, and the background pressure, which either lets the vapor plume expand or confines it.

The crystals came out with selenium vacancy levels tunable from about 1% to 20%, uniform across each flake. Raman spectroscopy turned into a fast, nondestructive gauge of that number: as vacancies increase, the A₁g peak slides from ~240 down to ~233 cm⁻¹ and a defect band grows in. Collaborators at Oak Ridge National Laboratory imaged the crystals atom by atom with scanning transmission electron microscopy and confirmed the count (mostly single missing selenium atoms), while atomic force microscopy showed they remained clean 0.7 nm monolayers. The crystals' glow shifts with vacancy level, and their excitons live measurably longer as the defects multiply.

The point is that the imperfections are grown in rather than blasted in, so the lattice stays intact. That turns a crystal's flaw into a dial: crank up the vacancies for stronger molecular binding in gas and biosensors, or turn them down for cleaner optoelectronics, and check every batch with Raman without destroying it.
